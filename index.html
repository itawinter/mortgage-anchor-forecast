Method

how these numbers are produced

A zero-coupon curve z(t) prices money out to every maturity today. Two points on it pin down the rate the market is implicitly quoting for a loan that starts at T and runs for L years — the forward rate. No-arbitrage forces it:

f(T, L) = (1 + z(T+L))T+L(1 + z(T))T1/L − 1

For a variable track, the anchor at each reset is a government yield for a term equal to the reset interval. So the anchor expected N months from now is f(N/12, L/12) with L the reset interval. At N = 0 this collapses to the spot rate z(L/12), which is why the table's first row is today.

Calibration. Your contract's stated anchor rarely equals z(L) exactly — banks read the anchor off a trailing average of published yields over a defined lookback, so it lags the live curve, and the precise definition varies by lender under Proper Conduct of Banking Business Directive 451. With Anchor to contract on, the calculator computes offset = your anchor − z(L) and carries that constant offset across every horizon. You get a path that starts exactly where your contract actually is, and moves the way the curve says it should. Raw curve drops the offset and shows the unadjusted forwards.

The slope adjustment. A forward rate is the expected future rate plus a term premium, and the premium grows with how far ahead you look. So an upward-sloping curve lifts every projected anchor even if the market expects no rate change at all — the raw path inherits the curve's shape, not just its expectations. The Adjusted column subtracts an estimate of that mechanical component. Three ways to estimate it, all zero at today's row, because today's anchor is observed rather than forecast:

  • Long-end slope (default) — deduct λ · T, with λ the annualised slope of the curve's far end. Past roughly ten years, expected policy changes are small, so residual slope out there is mostly premium accrual. On the loaded curve that is about 5 bp per year, taken from the 10y→15y segment. Its virtue is that nothing is hand-tuned: the number comes from your own curve.
  • Fixed premium — deduct a flat bp · T you supply, when you have your own view or a published term-premium estimate.
  • Shrink toward today — keep a fraction of the projected rise: adjusted = raw − θ · (raw − today). At θ = 1 the path is flat at today's anchor, which is the random-walk forecast. This is the bluntest method and the best supported empirically: forwards are biased predictors of future rates, and a random walk is a stubbornly hard benchmark to beat.

These are transparent heuristics, not a term-structure model — a proper decomposition (Adrian-Crump-Moench, Kim-Wright) fits a factor model to the whole curve and yields a premium that varies by both horizon and maturity. Treat the raw and adjusted columns as bracketing the answer: raw is the rate you could lock in today, adjusted is closer to what a rate-setter's path implies. The truth usually sits between them, and the gap between the two is the honest measure of how much of the projection is premium rather than expectation.

Linked vs unlinked. CPI-linked tracks are read off the real (Galil) curve, so their anchors are real rates — principal and payment are indexed on top. The gap between the two curves at the same horizon is breakeven inflation — the market's price for taking indexation risk. The table reports it at each horizon; the Bank of Israel's 1–3% target band is the yardstick to read it against. It appears only once both legs are real data: computed against a placeholder curve it would be an artefact of an invented number.

Prime. The prime rate is the Bank of Israel rate plus 1.5pp by convention. Its forward path is taken from the short end — the 3-month forward f(N/12, 0.25) — as a proxy for the expected policy rate, then 1.5pp is added. Calibration is against the current prime you enter.

Conventions. Zero rates are read as annual-effective. Monthly instalments use the Israeli quoting convention of a nominal annual rate divided by twelve. Outside the entered maturity range the curve is held flat rather than extrapolated.

The loaded curve. The unlinked curve splices two sources at one year. Out to 1y it is Makam — central bank bills, yields to maturity, read live from the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange listing, one point per bill on issue. From 2y to 15y it is the Bank of Israel's Nominal rate of return derived from the zero coupon yield curve estimation (Statistics/shcd08_e.xls), which publishes monthly averages as alternating month-end (Calendar) and mid-month (CPI-dated) rows. Averaged data suits anchor work, since contractual anchors are themselves read off averaged yields rather than a single day's fix. Each source's own date is listed against it in Data sources above.

Three things follow from that. The two sources carry different dates and averaging bases, so they do not agree exactly where they meet: the zero curve's 1y point reads a few bp below the 1y bill. Makam governs the 0–1y span as the short-term instrument and the fresher observation — and it governs the whole span, not just the maturities it happens to quote. Bills mature on real dates, so the longest one usually stops short of a round year; where it does, the 1y point is interpolated from that bill to the 2y point rather than taking the older average's level. That matters more than it sounds: mixing the two at adjacent maturities puts a small dip in the curve, and a forward rate has to make that dip up, which turns a few bp of level difference into something that reads like a market view at the 12-month horizon. Anything past 15y is held flat, which the fixed long-term tracks flag. And the shortest bill sits well above its neighbours — days from redemption, the annualisation multiplier is enormous, so a rounding in its price is worth tens of bp. It is left in as published rather than quietly smoothed, but treat the very front of the curve with care.

The CPI-linked curve is the matching Real rate of return derived from the zero coupon yield curve estimation (Statistics/shcd07_e.xls) — the Galil curve, same workbook family, same monthly-average basis, 1y to 20y; its own observation date is listed against it in Data sources above. It is characteristically humped at the front — dipping from 1y to around 3y before rising again, and the plotted curve shows where it sits today. With that shape a CPI-linked variable track projects a falling real anchor over the next couple of years, the opposite of the unlinked one — the market expects real short rates to come down. Below 1y it is held flat, which matters for the breakeven column at short horizons, and beyond 20y the long fixed linked tracks flag it.

The two legs are observed on different dates — the nominal curve's short end is a daily Makam print, the real curve a mid-month average, typically a fortnight apart. Breakeven inflation is the difference between them, so it carries that timing mismatch. Each leg's own date is shown against it rather than a single date for the page, because one number there would misstate the older leg.

A forward rate is a price, not a forecast. It is where the market will let you lock a future rate today, and it embeds a term premium — so in an upward-sloping curve forwards systematically sit above the average outcome that actually materialises. Read these as the market-implied path, not as a prediction of Bank of Israel policy. Nothing here is financial advice.
